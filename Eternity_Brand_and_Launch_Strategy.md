# Eternity Products
## Brand, Product & Launch Strategy for Nepal

**Cosmetics, professional styling tools and salon equipment**
Built August 2026 · Kathmandu, Nepal

---

## 1. Executive summary

Eternity Products sells Ikonic professional styling tools, cosmetics and salon equipment in Nepal through TikTok and Facebook, supplying both retail buyers and an existing base of beauty parlours. The goods are imported directly from India.

Research for this document pulled the complete Ikonic India catalogue (320 SKUs with list and MRP pricing) and every Ikonic listing on Daraz Nepal, then matched them against each other. That comparison produced one finding that should reshape how the business allocates its time.

**The business has two product lines with completely different economics, and they are currently being treated as one.**

| | Traffic line | Profit line |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | Straighteners, dryers, curlers, stylers | Barber chairs, shampoo stations, spa beds, trolleys, steamers |
| **Daraz Nepal presence** | Heavy. Every price visible | **None.** Searches return office chairs and consumer gadgets |
| **Margin** | 25–30% | ~50% |
| **Profit per unit** | NPR ~2,900 | NPR ~94,000 |
| **Who buys** | Retail consumers | Salon owners |
| **What it is for** | Audience, trust, cash flow | Actual profit |

A single six-chair salon fit-out produces roughly **NPR 563,000** of gross profit. Matching that on straighteners takes **193 units sold**.

Both lines come from the same supplier. Ikonic manufactures the barber chairs, shampoo stations and spa beds as well as the straighteners — one distributor relationship already covers the entire opportunity.

**The strategic conclusion:** the styling tools are not the business. They are the marketing channel for the business. They build the audience, prove authenticity, and generate the salon relationships. The salon equipment is where the money is, and it faces no marketplace price competition in Nepal at all.

## 2. The company

### What Eternity Products is

An importer and distributor of professional hair styling tools, cosmetics and salon equipment, operating in Nepal and sourcing directly from India.

The business runs on two engines at once. It sells styling tools to consumers through social commerce, and it supplies beauty parlours directly with tools, consumables and salon furniture. Most competitors do one or the other. Doing both is the structural advantage: the consumer side builds the audience and the brand, and the trade side converts that standing into high-value equipment orders.

### Where it operates today

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sourcing** | Direct import from India. Ikonic is the anchor brand, plus Silk Hair and Qween |
| **Retail channels** | TikTok `@eternity.products`, Facebook. Order taken by DM, fulfilled cash on delivery |
| **Trade channel** | Direct supply to beauty parlours, relationship-led, no formal portal yet |
| **Product range** | Straighteners, dryers, curlers, crimpers, stylers, professional scissors, salon chairs, manicure and pedicure stations, a 7-in-1 hydrafacial machine |
| **Market** | Kathmandu valley core, with delivery beyond it |

### What the business already owns

These are assets, and they are worth naming because the strategy depends on them.

**A distributor relationship covering the entire opportunity.** Ikonic manufactures both the styling tools and the salon furniture. One supplier agreement reaches from a NPR 1,554 blow-dry brush to a NPR 200,000 pedicure station. Most distributors have to assemble that range from several suppliers.

**An installed base of parlour customers.** Salons already buying regularly. This is recurring revenue with the relationship already made — the hardest and slowest part of B2B, already done.

**Direct import capability.** Buying at India distributor prices rather than through a local wholesaler is what creates the margin corridor in section 3. It also means the price is set, not accepted.

**A social audience being built on owned channels.** TikTok and Facebook presence under a consistent brand name.

**Category knowledge.** Knowing which products salons actually reach for, and why, is not something a competitor can order from a catalogue.

### What the business is building toward

1. **An own website** — to stop renting the customer relationship from a marketplace, and to price B2B privately
2. **An own brand** — Eternity-labelled products alongside the distributed lines
3. **A warehouse and distribution operation** — supplying other retailers, not only end customers

That third goal is the real destination. The first two are how it gets funded and de-risked.

### Where the business sits in the chain

```
Ikonic (India, manufacturer)
        │  direct import, distributor pricing
        ▼
ETERNITY PRODUCTS  ── importer + distributor
        │                    │
        │ D2C retail         │ B2B trade
        ▼                    ▼
   Consumers            Salons & parlours
   (social, COD)        (relationship, credit)
                             │
                             ▼
                    Their retail customers
```

The long-term position is one step further left: becoming the layer other Nepali retailers buy from, rather than only selling through.

## 4. Ideal customer profile

Four segments matter, and they are not equally valuable. They are ranked here by gross profit per relationship, not by headcount — because the largest segment by volume is the least valuable per customer, and treating them as equally important is how selling time gets misallocated.

### ICP 1 — The established salon owner *(primary, highest value)*

**This is the profile the business should be built around.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Owns a running parlour or salon, 3–8 chairs, 2+ years trading |
| **Where** | Kathmandu valley first: Baneshwor, Chabahil, Kalanki, Lalitpur, Bhaktapur. Then Pokhara, Butwal, Chitwan, Biratnagar |
| **Buys** | Tools in bulk, consumables monthly, furniture and equipment in single high-value units |
| **Order value** | NPR 6,000 monthly consumables → NPR 195,000 single chair → NPR 500,000+ full fit-out |
| **Gross profit** | ~50% on equipment. One fit-out ≈ NPR 563,000 |
| **Decision maker** | The owner, personally. Never a purchasing department |
| **Buying trigger** | Adding a chair, moving premises, a competitor upgrading nearby, festival/wedding season revenue arriving |
| **Buying process** | Relationship first, then trial, then a site visit. Equipment never closes over the phone |
| **Where they are** | Facebook groups, Viber, and physically in their shop. Not on LinkedIn |
| **Primary objection** | *"Yeti mahango chair kina?"* — they buy assets, so price alone is meaningless |
| **What converts** | Payback arithmetic: chair cost against monthly chair revenue. And a supplier who answers the phone |
| **Sell first** | A consumable or a single tool. Small, effortless, low risk. The equipment conversation comes at order three or four |

**Why this ICP wins:** they buy repeatedly, they buy the high-margin line, they refer other salons, and they are almost impossible for a marketplace to serve. The relationship is the moat.

### ICP 2 — The new salon opening *(highest value per event, hardest to time)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Opening a first or second location, often a stylist going independent |
| **When** | Concentrated around Baisakh (Nepali new year, April) and after festival season |
| **Buys** | Everything at once — chairs, shampoo station, trolleys, steamer, mirrors, full tool kit |
| **Order value** | NPR 400,000 – 1,200,000 in a single engagement |
| **Buying trigger** | Lease signed. From that moment there is a fixed deadline |
| **Decision driver** | Certainty and speed. They are terrified of opening late |
| **Primary objection** | *"Can they actually deliver a full setup on time?"* |
| **What converts** | A completed fit-out case study with photos, plus a written delivery and installation schedule |
| **Sell first** | The site visit and the measured plan. Free, and it makes you the default supplier |
| **How to find them** | New salon registrations, stylists announcing independence on social, landlords and interior contractors, and your existing salon network (they always know first) |

**Why this ICP is worth a dedicated motion:** a single event produces more gross profit than a year of retail. The difficulty is purely timing, which is why the existing salon community is the best early-warning system available.

### ICP 3 — The professional stylist *(bridge segment)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Working stylist, employed at a salon, buys their own kit |
| **Age** | 20–35 |
| **Buys** | Scissors (INR 1,155–5,785 India list), their own straightener or dryer, blow-dry brushes |
| **Order value** | NPR 3,000 – 15,000, several times a year |
| **Buying trigger** | Starting a job, a scissor going blunt, learning a new technique |
| **Decision driver** | Craft credibility. They want what good stylists use |
| **Primary objection** | *"Is this actually professional grade or just marketed that way?"* |
| **What converts** | Letting them hold it. Stylists buy on feel, and a scissor cannot be sold from a photo |
| **Channel** | In-salon visits, TikTok technique content, free training sessions |

**Why this segment matters more than its revenue:** stylists become salon owners. A stylist who trusts you at 24 is a fit-out customer at 30. They also influence what their current employer buys.

### ICP 4 — The retail consumer *(volume, thin margin, real strategic purpose)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Women 18–35, urban and semi-urban, styling at home |
| **Where** | TikTok and Facebook. Discovery is almost entirely social |
| **Buys** | One tool, occasionally a bundle. Straighteners, dryers, multi-stylers |
| **Order value** | NPR 2,700 – 14,200. Typically one purchase, sometimes a second as a gift |
| **Gross profit** | ~NPR 2,900 per unit at 25–30% |
| **Buying trigger** | A wedding, Teej, Dashain, a bad experience with a cheap tool, a reel |
| **Payment** | Cash on delivery, overwhelmingly |
| **Primary objection** | *"Original ho ki copy?"* — the single biggest blocker in this segment |
| **Secondary objections** | Heat damage fear, Daraz price comparison, delivery reliability outside the valley |
| **What converts** | Authenticity proof on camera, open-box-on-delivery, and a matched price with better service |

**Their strategic purpose is not the margin.** This segment builds the audience, generates the content proof, funds cash flow, and — critically — makes salon owners aware of the brand before any sales call happens. A salon owner who has seen your videos for three months is a warm conversation.

### Segment priority

| Rank | Segment | GP per relationship | Effort | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Established salon owner | Very high, recurring | Medium | **Build the business here** |
| 2 | New salon opening | Highest per event | High, timing-dependent | Dedicated motion, early-warning network |
| 3 | Professional stylist | Low now, high later | Low | Invest for the long game |
| 4 | Retail consumer | Low, one-off | Low per sale, high in aggregate | The marketing channel, not the destination |

### Who to say no to

Naming the anti-customer is as useful as naming the customer, because these consume the same selling time and return far less.

**The price-only shopper.** Opens with "last price kati?" before asking a single question about the product. They will leave for a NPR 200 difference and they generate the most support load. Match your published price and move on.

**Low-value orders far outside the valley.** A NPR 1,500 brush shipped to a remote district costs more in delivery, refusal risk and support than it earns. Set a minimum order value for outside-valley delivery, or bundle it.

**Anyone asking you to quote a chair over the phone.** Not because they are bad customers, but because that conversation cannot be won on a photo and a number. Convert it into a site visit or decline it.

**Resellers wanting distributor pricing without volume.** They compress your margin and then compete against you on your own products. Tier pricing exists so this is answered by policy, not by negotiation.

### What this means for where time goes

The 193-to-1 ratio from section 1 is the whole argument. One afternoon spent visiting three salons has a higher expected value than a week of retail order handling.

The retail line should be increasingly automated — website, content calendar, COD confirmation queue — precisely so that owner time can be spent on ICP 1 and ICP 2, which cannot be automated and where the margin actually is.

## 5. Brand positioning

### The problem Eternity solves

The Nepali beauty market has a trust problem, not a supply problem. Styling tools are widely available and cheap counterfeits are everywhere. The most common question a buyer asks is not "how much?" — it is **"original ho?"**

Salon owners have a different problem. They can buy tools locally but cannot easily buy professional equipment at all, and when they can, nobody explains whether it will pay for itself.

### Positioning statement

> For Nepali women who style at home and for salon owners building a business, Eternity Products is the supplier that proves what it sells is genuine and shows exactly what it will earn — because we import it ourselves and we stand behind it here, in Nepal.

### The three brand pillars

**Proven genuine.** Serial numbers, warranty cards, unboxing on camera. Authenticity is treated as a product feature, not a defensive answer.

**Local accountability.** Warranty honoured here, service here, a real person who answers. This is precisely what a marketplace seller cannot offer.

**Trade expertise.** Not a shop that happens to sell hair tools — the supplier the salon trade relies on, that also sells to consumers.

### What Eternity is not

Not the cheapest. Competing on price against Daraz means competing against sellers with no warranty obligation and no service cost. That is a race with no winner.

## 6. Pricing strategy

### The rule: match the anchor, win on everything else

For any product with a visible Daraz listing, price **at or within a few percent of** the Daraz price. Do not undercut. Undercutting starts a price war on a product where the margin is only 25–30%, and it signals that the product might not be genuine.

Win instead on:

- warranty honoured locally
- authenticity verification
- open-box-on-delivery
- service and advice after the sale
- delivery certainty

### For products with no Daraz listing

This is the salon equipment, and roughly two thirds of the catalogue. Price from landed cost plus target margin. There is no anchor to fight, so the constraint is what the customer can justify, not what a competitor charges.

For equipment, the justification is always **payback period**:

> "This chair costs X. Four customers a day earns Y a month. It pays for itself in Z months, then it is profit."

A salon owner who has done that arithmetic is no longer comparing prices.

### Never discount price on the profit line

Discount delivery, installation, a free trolley, an extra year of service. Never the chair. A price discount teaches the customer to negotiate on every future order and permanently resets the anchor.

## 7. Import economics

Goods come from India, which brings the SAFTA preference and the currency peg into play.

### The build-up

```
India invoice (INR)
  × 1.60                      NPR/INR peg
  = CIF value in NPR
  + customs duty              0–80% ad valorem, per HS line
  = duty-paid value
  + VAT 13%                   on (CIF + duty) — recoverable if VAT registered
  + clearing agent + docs
  + inland freight to warehouse
  = TRUE LANDED COST
```

### Three levers that materially change the number

**1. VAT registration.** VAT is charged on CIF plus duty. Registered, it is a recoverable pass-through. Unregistered, it is 13% of landed value gone permanently. On a NPR 100,000 chair that is roughly NPR 13,000 per unit. This is the single largest margin lever available and it is an administrative task, not a commercial negotiation.

**2. The SAFTA certificate of origin.** India-origin goods qualify for a duty reduction of roughly 5% where the base rate is 30% or under, and 7% above that — but only with a valid certificate of origin from the supplier. Make it a standing condition on every purchase order, not a per-shipment request.

**3. The distributor discount.** The India *list* price is not the buy price. Working backwards from a 25–30% margin on verified Daraz prices, the implied distributor discount is around **25% off list on tools**. Furniture normally carries a deeper discount; the workbook shows that hitting 50% on a barber chair at NPR 195,000 requires about a 47% discount off list. Confirming real terms against these figures is worth doing before the next order.

### Worked example — Ikonic Gleam 3.0

| | NPR |
|---|---|
| Selling price (matches Daraz) | 12,920 |
| Landed cost | 9,424 |
| Packaging | 120 |
| Delivery, inside valley | 150 |
| Cost of COD refusals, spread | 41 |
| COD collection fee | 258 |
| **Net profit per delivered unit** | **2,927** |
| Gross margin | 27.1% |

Sold through Daraz instead, commission and payout fees cut net profit to NPR 2,359. **Owning the checkout is worth NPR 568 per unit** — meaningful at volume, and the entire financial case for building the website.

### Worked example — Ikonic Barber Chair Felix

|---|---|

| Delivery and installation | 3,500 |
| **Net profit per chair** | **93,926** |

| Straighteners needed to match it | **193** |

## 8. Channel strategy

### Where each channel belongs

**TikTok** is the discovery engine. TikTok Shop and creator monetisation are not available in Nepal, so it cannot close a sale — it must hand off to DM or the website. That is a constraint to design around, not a reason to use it less.

**Facebook** carries the older salon-owner demographic and is where parlour communities actually talk. Facebook groups matter more here than the page.

**Daraz** is a discovery channel for price-transparent tools, and a competitor everywhere else. Use it to be found; migrate repeat buyers to owned channels. Never list the salon equipment there — it would create the price anchor that currently does not exist.

**The website** is where margin is protected, B2B pricing can be tiered privately, and inventory connects to the warehouse.

**Viber** is the retention engine. Nepal runs on Viber, and a broadcast list is owned audience that no algorithm can throttle.

### Payment reality

| Method | Status |
|---|---|
| Cash on delivery | Works. The default. Budget the refusal rate honestly |
| eSewa, Khalti, Fonepay | Work. Merchant onboarding needs PAN |
| Bank transfer | Works. Standard for salon fit-outs |
| Stripe, PayPal | **Cannot receive in Nepal.** Do not design around them |

## 9. Marketing psychology for this market

Generic persuasion tactics are not what moves a Nepali buyer. These are the specific hesitations, and the responses that address them.

### Authenticity anxiety — the biggest single blocker

**They think:** *"Original ho ki copy?"*

Counterfeit styling tools are everywhere, and most buyers have been burned or know someone who was.

**The move:** make verification a feature. Serial number, warranty card and box seal on camera in every listing and every proof video.

> *"Serial number cha, warranty card cha, box seal cha. Video ma sabai dekhaeko cha — check garera matra kinnus."*

### The COD trust gap

**They think:** *"Paisa dida saman thik nabhaye?"*

**The move:** open-box-on-delivery. It converts hesitation into orders and cuts refusal rates.

> *"Rider agadi box kholnus. Thik chaina bhane nalinus — paisa tirnu pardaina."*

### Price anchoring against Daraz

**They think:** *"Daraz ma sasto cha ni"*

**The move:** match the price, then move the comparison to ground where a marketplace seller cannot follow.

> *"Price ustai ho. Farak: warranty hami afai dinchhau, servicing local cha, ra original guarantee."*

### Salon owner ROI doubt

**They think:** *"Yeti mahango chair kina?"*

Salon owners buy assets. A price with no earning figure beside it is meaningless to them.

> *"Chair ko price X. Din ko 4 customer ma mahina Y kamai. Z mahina ma paisa uthchha — tyaspachi profit."*

### Fear of heat damage

**They think:** *"Kapal bigrincha ki"*

Genuine and widespread. Education converts better than any discount in this category.

> *"Patlo kapal 160–180, normal 180–200, bakhrilo 200–230. Yo tool ma control cha — tyahi ho farak."*

### The festival cash cycle

Household spending concentrates around Dashain and Tihar bonuses. Take a small deposit before the peak and deliver during it.

> *"Ahile NPR 1,000 booking, Dashain ma delivery. Price aajai ko lock huncha."*

## 10. The content system

### The principle

Consistency beats production value. Four adequate posts a week beat one polished post a month, and the calendar has to survive a bad week.

### Three capacity tiers

The 90-day calendar is written with every row tagged Solo, Plus1 or Team. Filter to current capacity and do only those rows. Nothing needs rewriting when capacity changes.

- **Solo** — 3–4 posts/week, phone only, one batch-shoot day
- **Plus1** — daily posting with one helper
- **Team** — daily plus paid amplification and UGC

### The batch-shoot system

One fixed day each week. Same light, same background, same table. Six to eight clips in 60–90 minutes, one take each. Captions written in bulk from the hooks sheet, then scheduled.

The fixed day matters more than which day. Perfectionism is why content calendars die in week three.

### Content pillars

| Pillar | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Proof | Kills authenticity anxiety. The single most important pillar |
| Transformation | Before/after. Demonstrable, not claimed |
| Education | Builds authority and earns saves |
| Social proof | Named local salons and customers. Anonymous reads as fake here |
| Offer | Real deadlines, real stock counts. Never fake scarcity |
| Behind the brand | Stock arriving, packing, delivery runs. Proof of a real trade |
| B2B | Fit-out reveals with payback maths on screen |
| Comparison | Cheap copy versus the real tool. Loss aversion |
| Community | UGC reposts under a campaign hashtag |

### Festival anchoring

Beauty and salon demand in Nepal is seasonal and the seasons are predictable. Dashain is the single largest window and needs stock ordered roughly six weeks ahead. Wedding season is when salons have cash and urgency — that is the B2B window.

Festival dates follow the Bikram Sambat lunar calendar and shift annually. Confirm against the patro before committing spend.

## 11. The salon community engine

This is the most valuable and most underexploited asset in the business.

There is already a base of parlours buying regularly. That is not a lead list — it is recurring revenue with a relationship already established. Most of it currently lives in a phone and in memory.

### What it becomes

A registered community with:

- an account record per salon: contact, area, chair count, purchase history, tier
- tier pricing that rewards volume without ad-hoc negotiation
- first access to new stock, as the concrete benefit of membership
- broadcast for the group, personal nudges for individuals
- quarterly free training

### The broadcast discipline

A broadcast is for the group. A reorder nudge is for one named person. Mixing them is how a valuable list becomes a muted one.

Four useful messages for every one that sells. The list stops working the moment it becomes a sales channel only.

### Why training is the moat

A salon that gets free technique training, first access to stock and someone who answers the phone does not switch supplier over a 5% price difference. Equipment and consumables are commodities; the relationship is not.

### The pipeline

Identified → Contacted → Sampled → First order → Repeat → Equipment → Fit-out partner → Advocate

The critical transition is **Repeat → Equipment**. That is where a NPR 6,000 customer becomes a NPR 200,000 customer, and it only happens through a site visit. Furniture never closes over the phone — quoting a chair price by text loses to a photo from a cheaper supplier every time.

## 13. The ecommerce and warehouse system

The full technical specification is in `ANTIGRAVITY_BUILD_PROMPT.md`. The commercial requirements it has to satisfy:

**Storefront** — catalogue and product pages built around authenticity proof, COD as a first-class payment method alongside eSewa and Khalti, delivery pricing that distinguishes inside from outside the valley, and Daraz price shown as an anchor where one exists.

**Admin** — a COD confirmation queue (the call before dispatch is what controls the refusal rate), stock across multiple locations, purchase orders and goods receipts, and margin reporting that is permission-gated so cost data does not leak to staff who negotiate.

**B2B portal** — salon accounts with tier pricing, quote requests, credit terms for qualifying accounts only, and reorder history.

**Community module** — broadcast to opted-in salons, new-arrival and restock announcements, and triggered reorder nudges.

Built in that order. Each phase ships something usable rather than waiting on the whole system.

## 14. The 90-day plan

**Weeks 1–2 — cost nothing, de-risk everything**
Confirm VAT registration status and register if needed. Load real supplier costs into the workbook. Load existing parlour accounts into the CRM sheet and collect Viber broadcast opt-in. First batch-shoot. Film the authenticity proof video.

**Weeks 3–4 — set the commercial foundations**
Set the price ladder against the verified Daraz anchors. Make the SAFTA certificate of origin a standing PO condition. Call the ten highest-value salon accounts and book three site visits.

**Weeks 5–8 — build the owned channel**
